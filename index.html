Section 1: Concept & Core Principles

Definitions, coordinate algebraic formulas, and geometric properties.

A geometric transformation is an operation that maps an original figure, called the preimage, onto a new figure, called the image. When a transformation preserves both distance (side lengths) and angle measures, it is classified as a rigid motion (or isometry). Rigid motions never stretch or distort a shape; therefore, the preimage and image are guaranteed to be congruent (\(\cong\)).

📐 Mathematical Coordinate Rules:

Distance Invariance
\[AB = A'B'\]
The length of any segment in the image is strictly equal to the length in the preimage.
Angle Invariance
\[m\angle A = m\angle A'\]
All corresponding angle measures remain identical.
Parallelism Invariance
\[AB \parallel CD \iff A'B' \parallel C'D'\]
Lines that were parallel in the preimage remain parallel in the image.

⚖️ Geometric Property Invariance:

Geometric Property Preservation Status
Side Lengths (Distance) ✓ Preserved
Angle Measures ✓ Preserved
Parallelism of Lines ✓ Preserved
Collinearity of Points ✓ Preserved
Perimeter & Area ✓ Identical (P'=P, A'=A)
Orientation Reversed ONLY by reflection

📚 Essential Vocabulary Cards:

Transformation
A function that changes the position, orientation, or size of a shape on a coordinate plane.
Example: Translations, reflections, rotations, and dilations.
Rigid Motion (Isometry)
A transformation that preserves distances and angle measures, keeping size and shape identical.
Example: Translations, reflections, and rotations.
Preimage & Image
The preimage is the original starting shape; the image is the resulting shape after the transformation.
Example: Preimage \(\triangle ABC\) maps to image \(\triangle A'B'C'\).
Orientation
The order of vertices (clockwise vs. counterclockwise) or direction a figure faces.
Example: Translations and rotations preserve orientation; reflections reverse it.

Section 1: Concept & Core Principles

Definitions, coordinate algebraic formulas, and geometric properties.

A translation is a rigid motion that slides every point of a figure the exact same distance and in the exact same direction along a straight line vector \(\vec{v} = \langle a, b \rangle\). In coordinate notation: \((x, y) \to (x + a, y + b)\). When \(a > 0\), the figure shifts right; when \(a < 0\), it shifts left. When \(b > 0\), the figure shifts up; when \(b < 0\), it shifts down.

📐 Mathematical Coordinate Rules:

Algebraic Translation Rule
\[(x, y) \to (x + a, y + b)\]
Add horizontal shift 'a' to x; add vertical shift 'b' to y.
Parallel Segment Property
\[AA' \parallel BB' \parallel CC'\]
Segments connecting corresponding preimage and image points are parallel and equal in length: \(AA' = BB' = CC' = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\).

Geometric Property Preservation Status
Side Lengths (Distance) ✓ Preserved
Angle Measures ✓ Preserved
Orientation ✓ Preserved (Same direction)
Slope of Segments ✓ Preserved (Parallel lines)
Area & Perimeter ✓ Preserved

Translation
A transformation that slides every point of a figure by a specified horizontal and vertical distance without turning or flipping.
Example: Sliding a shape 4 units right and 3 units down.
Vector \(\langle a, b \rangle\)
A quantity specifying both direction and distance of movement.
Example: \(\langle -3, 5 \rangle\) means 3 units left and 5 units up.
Prime Notation
The symbol (') appended to letters denoting image vertices.
Example: Point \(A(2, 3)\) translates to \(A'(6, 1)\).

Section 1: Concept & Core Principles

Definitions, coordinate algebraic formulas, and geometric properties.

A reflection is a rigid motion that flips a figure across a specific line called the line of reflection. Each point and its image are the exact same distance from the line of reflection, and the segment connecting them is perpendicular to that line. The line of reflection acts as the perpendicular bisector of every segment connecting a preimage point to its image point.

📐 Mathematical Coordinate Rules:

Across the x-axis
\[(x, y) \to (x, -y)\]
x stays the same; y changes sign (negates).
Across the y-axis
\[(x, y) \to (-x, y)\]
x changes sign (negates); y stays the same.
Across the line y = x
\[(x, y) \to (y, x)\]
Swap the x and y coordinates.
Across vertical line x = k
\[(x, y) \to (2k - x, y)\]
Horizontal flip across vertical line x = k.

⚖️ Geometric Property Invariance:

Geometric Property Preservation Status
Side Lengths & Angles ✓ Preserved (Congruent)
Parallelism of Lines ✓ Preserved
Perimeter & Area ✓ Preserved
Vertex Orientation ✕ Reversed (Mirror flip)

📚 Essential Vocabulary Cards:

Reflection
A transformation that flips a figure across a line creating a mirror image.
Example: Looking in a mirror or flipping over the x-axis.
Line of Reflection
The line across which a figure is reflected. It is the perpendicular bisector of every segment connecting corresponding points.
Example: The x-axis (y = 0) or the line y = x.
Reversed Orientation
The reversal of vertex order from clockwise to counterclockwise.
Example: Clockwise \(\triangle ABC\) becomes counterclockwise \(\triangle A'B'C'\).

Section 1: Concept & Core Principles

Definitions, coordinate algebraic formulas, and geometric properties.

A rotation is a rigid motion that turns every point of a figure through a specified angle and direction about a fixed point called the center of rotation. When rotating about the origin \((0, 0)\), every point \(P\) moves along a circular arc such that \(OP = OP'\). Rotations preserve side lengths, angle measures, and vertex orientation.

📐 Mathematical Coordinate Rules:

90° Clockwise (270° CCW)
\[(x, y) \to (y, -x)\]
Swap coordinates, then negate the new y.
180° Turn (Half-turn)
\[(x, y) \to (-x, -y)\]
Negate both coordinates; direction does not matter.
270° Clockwise (90° CCW)
\[(x, y) \to (-y, x)\]
Swap coordinates, then negate the new x.
360° Full Turn
\[(x, y) \to (x, y)\]
Complete turn returns shape to original position.

Geometric Property Preservation Status
Distance to Center (Radius) ✓ Preserved (OP = OP')
Side Lengths & Angles ✓ Preserved
Orientation ✓ Preserved (Clockwise stays CW)
Area & Perimeter ✓ Preserved

Section 1: Concept & Core Principles

Definitions, coordinate algebraic formulas, and geometric properties.

The Fundamental Congruence Theorem: Two two-dimensional geometric figures are congruent (\(\cong\)) if and only if there exists a sequence of rigid motions that maps the first figure directly onto the second figure. When figures are congruent, all corresponding side lengths are equal, all corresponding angles are equal, and perimeter and area are identical.

📐 Mathematical Coordinate Rules:

Definition of Congruence
\[\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF \iff \text{Rigid Sequence maps } ABC \to DEF\]
Existence of a rigid motion sequence is the exact mathematical proof of congruence.
Corresponding Parts Property
\[AB = DE, BC = EF, AC = DF \text{ and } \angle A \cong \angle D, \angle B \cong \angle E, \angle C \cong \angle F\]
Corresponding Parts of Congruent Figures are Congruent (CPCTC).

⚖️ Geometric Property Invariance:

Geometric Property Preservation Status
All Side Lengths ✓ 100% Equal
All Angle Measures ✓ 100% Equal
Perimeter & Area ✓ Identical
Shape & Size ✓ Identical

📚 Essential Vocabulary Cards:

Congruent Figures (\(\cong\))
Figures that have the exact same size and shape.
Example: Two identical puzzle pieces.
Sequence of Transformations
Performing two or more transformations in succession.
Example: Translate 3 units right, then reflect across the x-axis.
Glide Reflection
A composition consisting of a translation along a line followed by a reflection across that same line.
Example: Footprint patterns in sand.

Section 1: Concept & Core Principles

Definitions, coordinate algebraic formulas, and geometric properties.

A dilation is a transformation that changes the size of a figure but maintains its overall shape and angle measures. A dilation is governed by two parameters: a fixed point called the center of dilation and a numerical ratio called the scale factor \(k\). If \(k > 1\), the figure grows into an enlargement. If \(0 < k < 1\), the figure shrinks into a reduction.

📐 Mathematical Coordinate Rules:

Scale Factor Formula
\[k = \frac{\text{Image Dimension}}{\text{Preimage Dimension}} = \frac{\text{NEW}}{\text{OLD}}\]
Always divide the image measurement by the preimage measurement.
Enlargement Criterion
\[k > 1\]
Image is larger than the preimage.
Reduction Criterion
\[0 < k < 1\]
Image is smaller than the preimage.

⚖️ Geometric Property Invariance:

Geometric Property Preservation Status
Angle Measures ✓ Preserved (Angles stay same!)
Geometric Shape ✓ Preserved
Side Lengths ✕ Changed by factor k
Perimeter ✕ Multiplied by k
Area ✕ Multiplied by k²

📚 Essential Vocabulary Cards:

Dilation
A non-rigid transformation that expands or shrinks a figure proportionally.
Example: Using a photocopier zoom function.
Scale Factor (k)
The ratio of corresponding linear dimensions of image to preimage.
Example: k = 2 doubles side lengths; k = 0.5 halves side lengths.
Center of Dilation
The fixed point from which all points are expanded or contracted.
Example: The origin (0, 0).

Origin Dilation Rule
\[(x, y) \to (kx, ky)\]

Section 1: Concept & Core Principles

Definitions, coordinate algebraic formulas, and geometric properties.

The Fundamental Similarity Theorem: Two figures are similar (\(\sim\)) if and only if there exists a sequence of transformations (combining rigid motions and at least one dilation) that maps the first figure onto the second. For similar figures: all corresponding angles are congruent (\(\cong\)) and all corresponding side lengths are in constant proportion (\(k\)). Most importantly: Perimeter scales by \(k\), while Area scales by \(k^2\)!

📐 Mathematical Coordinate Rules:

Angle Congruence Condition
\[\angle A \cong \angle A', \angle B \cong \angle B', \angle C \cong \angle C'\]
All corresponding angles must be congruent.
Side Proportionality Condition
\[\frac{A'B'}{AB} = \frac{B'C'}{BC} = \frac{A'C'}{AC} = k\]
All corresponding side length ratios must equal the exact same scale factor k.
Linear Perimeter Scaling Law
\[P' = k \cdot P\]
Perimeter scales linearly with the scale factor k.
Quadratic Area Scaling Law
\[A' = k^2 \cdot A\]
Area scales with the SQUARE of the scale factor k².

⚖️ Geometric Property Invariance:

Geometric Property Preservation Status
Angle Measures ✓ Strictly Congruent
Geometric Shape ✓ Identical Shape
Side Lengths Proportional (Ratio = k)
Perimeter Ratio P' / P = k
Area Ratio A' / A = k²

📚 Essential Vocabulary Cards:

Similar Figures (\(\sim\))
Figures that have the same shape, congruent angles, and proportional side lengths.
Example: An architectural model and the completed skyscraper.
Similarity Transformation
A composition of a dilation and one or more rigid motions.
Example: Dilate by k = 2, then rotate 90° clockwise.
Area Ratio (k²)
The ratio of image area to preimage area equals scale factor squared.
Example: If k = 3, area is 3² = 9 times larger.

How to Find Scale Factor \(k\):
\[k = \frac{\text{Image Side Length}}{\text{Preimage Side Length}} = \frac{x'}{x} = \frac{y'}{y}\]

Always remember: "NEW over OLD" (Image divided by Preimage)!

The Area Squaring Rule:
\[\text{Ratio of Perimeters} = k \quad\Big|\quad \text{Ratio of Areas} = k^2\]

If a square with Area = 5 is dilated by \(k = 4\), new Area = \(5 \times 4^2 = 5 \times 16 = 80\) sq units!
